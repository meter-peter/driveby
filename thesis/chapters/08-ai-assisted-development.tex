\chapter{AI-Assisted Development: Authorship, Process, and Reflection}
\label{ch:ai-development}

This thesis was conceived, designed, evaluated, and written by the author. It was \emph{built} with substantial help from an AI agent during a focused twelve-day productisation sprint near the end of the project. This chapter draws that distinction precisely: it assesses authorship (Section~\ref{sec:authorship}), describes the knowledge protocol and persistent memory behind the human--agent collaboration (Sections~\ref{sec:claudemd-protocol} and~\ref{sec:agent-memory}), and closes with reflection and the relation of the framework to autonomous-agent feedback loops (Sections~\ref{sec:agent-eval}--\ref{sec:personal-lessons}).

The chapter makes three claims: the author's intellectual ownership---over the SDT framework, the XSDLC architecture, the evaluation design, and the thesis narrative---is traceable through the version-control record; the author-designed CLAUDE.md knowledge protocol is what allowed an AI agent to accelerate implementation without dictating direction; and SDT's structured output positions it as a natural feedback source for autonomous-agent loops, to be tested further as future work (Section~\ref{sec:agent-feedback-future}).

\section{Authorship and the Author's Role}
\label{sec:authorship}
% ============================================================================

This section answers who did what in this thesis. Every claim below is reproducible from the project's git history, the persistent agent-memory directory at \texttt{\textasciitilde/.claude/\allowbreak projects/\allowbreak -home-meter-peter-\allowbreak development-driveby/}, and the sixteen \texttt{CLAUDE.md} files in the monorepo.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Two Phases of Development}
\label{subsec:two-phases}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The repository's commit history makes a clean distinction between two phases.

\textbf{Phase~A --- Solo development (3 April 2025 -- 18 March 2026, 349 days).} During this phase the author worked alone. The DDT/SDT axioms were drafted, the nine principles were enumerated, the \texttt{APISpec} abstraction was designed, the first principle checkers were written, the OpenAPI~3.x and Swagger~2.0 adapters were built, the validation modes were specified, the monorepo was restructured, the five evaluation APIs were designed and implemented, and the Crossplane composition was prototyped. All twenty-two commits in this phase carry no \texttt{Co-Authored-By} trailer; they are entirely the author's work. No generative AI assistance was used in this phase---neither an agentic tool nor a Copilot-style IDE assistant; development used a conventional IDE whose code intelligence was limited to non-generative tooling (symbol completion, navigation, linting).

\textbf{Phase~B --- Author-directed AI partnership (19--30 March 2026, 12 days).} During this phase the author engaged Claude Code~\cite{claude-code} intensively for productisation: the principle checking was finalised and DriveBy was brought to its released form, the GitOps pipeline was wired together, the multi-API evaluation tooling was developed, the XSDLC composition was hardened, and the thesis manuscript was drafted under the author's chapter specifications and feed map (Section~\ref{sec:agent-thesis}). Twenty-six of the fifty-two commits in the project carry the \texttt{Co-Authored-By: Claude} trailer; all fall in this 12-day window. Human-only commits continued throughout this phase---the last (2026-03-29) one day before the final commit---so the author kept making manual code changes throughout the partnership.

This timeline matters. The conceptual content of the thesis---the axioms, the principles, the architecture, the evaluation design---predates the AI partnership by nearly a year. The AI's role was not to invent the framework; it was to accelerate the implementation and writing of a framework that already existed in design.

\subsection{Overall Authorship Claim and Role}
\label{subsec:authorship-claim}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Combining the conceptual ownership (the framework, the architecture, the evaluation design, the thesis report structure), the quantitative line-level evidence (approximately 74\%---73.7\% of lines added and 73.5\% of lines removed---authored solo, per the table above), the timeline (a year of solo work followed by 12 days of directed partnership), and the reviewer-of-record role for every artefact, the author claims overall authorship of approximately \textbf{80\%} of this thesis and the DriveBy project. The remaining 20\% is acknowledged AI co-authorship, concentrated in the Phase~B productisation surface and in initial drafts of several thesis sections. The author---not the agent---rewrote, corrected, and finalised the content of every chapter, so the final text of the thesis is in every case author-authored (Section~\ref{sec:agent-thesis}).

\textbf{Role in software-project terms.} The author's role maps most cleanly to \emph{architect-and-tech-lead}: defining the technical vision (the SDT axioms and principles), designing the system architecture (the \texttt{APISpec} abstraction, the principle-checker pattern, the XSDLC composition, the two-repo BYOCI model), establishing the five code-quality invariants, and reviewing every output before integration. During Phase~B the role expanded to explicit \emph{agent orchestrator}: deciding what to delegate, supplying the structured context the agent required, and accepting or rejecting each output. The author did not act primarily as an analyst or as an implementor.

\textbf{Did the original idea survive?} The original idea---a framework that derives validation rules from the API specification rather than from manually authored tests, with a CLI that integrates into a GitOps pipeline---was implemented in full. Two notable scope changes occurred during the project, both author-driven decisions made before Phase~B:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The framing was sharpened to a \emph{conceptual framework} rather than a complete formal methodology (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}; also reflected in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}). This is a sharpening of the original claim, not a deviation from it.
    \item The Kubernetes integration was redesigned mid-project from a single-repo model into the two-repo BYOCI architecture (Chapter~\ref{ch:workflow}). This change was driven by an architectural insight about source hydration that the author identified independently; it expanded the contribution rather than replacing it.
\end{itemize}

No deviation in the project was driven by the AI agent suggesting a more interesting pathway. The Phase~B partnership accelerated execution of the design that already existed; it did not redirect the design.

\subsection{Demarcation of Intellectual Contribution}
\label{subsec:contribution-boundary}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The quantitative evidence above answers ``how much'' was authored solo; this subsection answers ``what specifically,'' because in an academic defence the burden of separating one's own contribution from agent-assisted artefact production rests with the candidate.

\textbf{Author's intellectual contribution.} The conceptual and design content of this thesis, traceable to author-only commits and to design decisions made before or independently of any AI session:

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.25em}
\item The \textbf{SDT paradigm} as a whole---the three framework-wide axioms (Completeness, Determinism, Observability), the nine validation principles (P001--P009), the validation modes (\texttt{minimal}, \texttt{strict}, \texttt{test-ready}, \texttt{test-only}), and the explicit rejection of an axiom-to-principle partition in favour of axioms as framework-wide invariants (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}).
\item The \textbf{formal model of validation as parallel application and aggregation}, $f_p(s) = \mathrm{agg}(f_{p_1}(s), \dots, f_{p_n}(s))$, with the explicit non-composition claim and its three consequences for Determinism, modular modes, and concurrent evaluation (Section~\ref{subsec:fp-formalism}).
\item The \textbf{DriveBy architecture}: the \texttt{APISpec} abstraction layer, the atomic-principle design and \texttt{PrincipleChecker} interface, the deterministic engine and registry pattern, the structured-report contract (Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}).
\item The \textbf{XSDLC composition design}: a single declarative CRD reifying the full delivery pipeline, the two-repo BYOCI boundary, the per-environment overlay model, the promotion-chain mechanics (Chapters~\ref{ch:kubernetes} and~\ref{ch:workflow}).
\item The \textbf{two-tier closed-loop quality-control framing} of the gate: verdict tier (open-loop) versus platform tier (closed-loop, autonomous repair across the controller stack), and the claim that drift between gate evaluations is blocked by construction rather than detected after the fact (Section~\ref{subsec:controller-stack}).
\item The \textbf{evaluation methodology}: the four-arm design (controlled, large-scale, operational, agent-feedback), the principle-injection scheme against the five evaluation APIs, and the single-iteration agent-feedback experiment (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).
\item The \textbf{five project-wide code-quality invariants} (Section~\ref{subsec:quant-authorship}) and the \texttt{CLAUDE.md} hierarchy that operationalises them as constraints on every later agent-assisted edit (Section~\ref{sec:claudemd-protocol}).
\item The \textbf{thesis chapter structure} and the chapter-to-source-code feed map (\texttt{thesis/CLAUDE.md}), which together define what each chapter argues and which source artefacts it must remain faithful to.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Agent-assisted scope.} The agent contribution, concentrated in the 12-day Phase~B and bounded above by the author's review and acceptance of every committed output:

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.25em}
\item \textbf{Routine Go boilerplate} within the principle checkers, authored against the interface and types the author had already designed. The conceptual content of each checker (what to check, why, with what severity) was author-specified; the agent rendered it as Go.
\item \textbf{LaTeX typesetting and prose-level revisions}: first drafts of prose around author-supplied chapter outlines and feed-maps; the author reviewed, edited, and committed.
\item \textbf{Documentation scaffolding}: per-package \texttt{CLAUDE.md} stubs and READMEs, and the documentation-completeness CI check. The hierarchical protocol itself and the root \texttt{CLAUDE.md} are author-designed.
\item \textbf{Operational tooling}: batch-validation scripts for the APIs.guru harvest, log-parsing helpers, the evaluation-table harness, and the per-PR comment formatter. The evaluation methodology these scripts serve is author-designed.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The boundary in one line.} Every architectural, formal, and methodological decision in this thesis was authored, reviewed, and committed by the candidate. Agent assistance is a development methodology---a way of accelerating the realisation of a design that already exists---not a substitute for the conceptual contribution that defines what is being realised.

\section{The CLAUDE.md Knowledge Protocol}
\label{sec:claudemd-protocol}
% ============================================================================

The central enabling infrastructure for the Phase~B partnership---and the author's most reusable methodological contribution from the agentic-development framework (Contribution~6 in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction})---is a hierarchical system of \texttt{CLAUDE.md} files distributed across the monorepo. These are neither READMEs nor configuration but agent context files: structured knowledge artefacts consumed by AI agents at the start of every session, providing the persistent context that enables cross-session continuity.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\label{subsec:hierarchy}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

When an agent begins working in a directory, the CLAUDE.md files in the path from root to that directory are loaded into its context---e.g.\ an agent modifying \texttt{driveby-cli/internal/principles/p004\_schema.go} receives the root, CLI, and principles files. This layered loading gives the agent both the global invariants and the local domain knowledge for the task at hand.

\textbf{Hierarchy depth and rationale.} The hierarchy is at most three levels deep (root $\to$ package $\to$ component), a trade-off chosen empirically during Phase~A: a flat structure left sessions without package-level context, while a five-level structure produced redundant content the agent's context window could not absorb without truncation.

\textbf{Containment vs.\ repetition.} Some sub-directory CLAUDE.md files repeat invariants from the root file. An alternative design would state each invariant exactly once in the highest applicable file and let layered loading propagate it downward; this design was considered and rejected in favour of repeating the five \emph{critical} code-quality rules in the leaf files. The justification is observational: in the Phase~A depth experiments, sessions demonstrably truncated ancestor content, whereas the file being actively edited was never truncated---the leaf file is the only placement observed to survive context-window pressure. The cost is duplication; the benefit is empirically needed robustness.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Content Categories}
\label{subsec:content-categories}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Each CLAUDE.md file contains four categories of knowledge:

\textbf{Structural knowledge}: what exists, where it lives, and how it connects---the root monorepo directory table, the CLI package dependency graph, the per-principle status table.

\textbf{Invariants}: rules that must never be violated. The root file specifies the five code quality rules: (1)~the mandatory \texttt{spec.APISpec} abstraction; (2)~the \texttt{PrincipleChecker} interface for every checker; (3)~no \texttt{os.Exit()} in command handlers; (4)~types confined to \texttt{internal/types/} with no circular dependencies; (5)~CLI configuration shared via \texttt{config.go} with no duplication. These are repeated in leaf files per the placement rationale of Section~\ref{subsec:hierarchy}.

\textbf{Procedures}: step-by-step instructions for common tasks---adding a new principle, building, testing, releasing---transforming tribal knowledge into structured, machine-executable instructions.

\textbf{Thesis mapping}: bidirectional traceability between code and academic writing. Every sub-directory CLAUDE.md names the thesis chapter describing that code; the thesis CLAUDE.md's Complete Feed Map maps every thesis section to its code and documentation sources. This enables both updating thesis content when code changes and updating code when thesis analysis reveals inconsistencies.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Maintenance Rules and Self-Enforcement}
\label{subsec:maintenance-rules}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The CLAUDE.md protocol includes four maintenance rules encoded in the root file:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item When modifying source files, update the corresponding CLAUDE.md in that directory.
    \item When modifying source files, update the corresponding \texttt{docs/} file if documented behaviour changes.
    \item When adding a new principle, create: source file, test file, \texttt{docs/principles/PXXX.md}, and update \texttt{principles/CLAUDE.md}.
    \item When adding a new directory, create a CLAUDE.md in it and add it to the root monorepo table.
\end{enumerate}

One clarification on the fourth rule: directories were planned up-front by the author during the monorepo restructuring, so the rule was exercised mainly as a verification step; it would matter more in projects where AI agents drive the directory layout.

These rules are not merely advisory: the CI pipeline includes a documentation completeness check (\texttt{tools/check-docs.sh}) performing nine categories of checks---CLAUDE.md existence, principle documentation completeness, documentation-versus-reality consistency (detecting stale claims like ``Not yet implemented'' when the source file exists), thesis chapter status, and test coverage gaps---and fails the build on any violation. This closes the loop: the protocol tells agents what to update, the CI check verifies updates were made, preventing knowledge drift---the same problem SDT prevents for API specifications, by the same means: treat documentation as a testable artefact and gate on its completeness.

\section{Persistent Agent Memory}
\label{sec:agent-memory}
% ============================================================================

Beyond the per-directory CLAUDE.md files, the project maintains a persistent memory system that survives across agent sessions. This memory captures information that cannot be derived from the codebase: user preferences, learned corrections, project decisions, and external references.

\textbf{Category of memory system.} In the taxonomy of memory architectures for AI systems, this is \emph{external, file-backed, semantic} memory: state lives outside the agent process in version-controllable Markdown files storing generalised knowledge---rules, preferences, project facts---rather than conversation transcripts. It is neither episodic (no per-session logs) nor procedural (procedures live in the CLAUDE.md files).

The memory directory contains twelve files: an index file (\texttt{MEMORY.md}) and eleven content entries.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{User profile} (1 file): the developer's role (diploma thesis student), expertise (deep Go and Kubernetes, new to LaTeX), and communication preferences.
    \item \textbf{Feedback corrections} (8 files): learned behaviours from iterative interaction, e.g.\ \texttt{feedback\_docs\_updates} (``always update CLAUDE.md, docs, and thesis drafts after each implementation session'') and \texttt{feedback\_runtime\_spec} (``sample APIs generate specs at runtime, not from static \texttt{openapi.json}''---a correction made after the agent assumed file-backed specs).
    \item \textbf{Project decisions} (1 file): the monorepo restructuring rationale.
    \item \textbf{External references} (1 file): the target cluster URL and the instruction to never reinstall ArgoCD.
\end{itemize}

The feedback corrections are the most active part of the system. Each encodes a lesson from a failed interaction, persisted so the same mistake would not recur; over Phase~B these accumulated into a behavioural profile that shaped the agent's approach. This is analogous to organisational learning in human teams~\cite{nonaka-knowledge}: tacit knowledge is externalised into explicit, persistent artefacts that new sessions inherit.

A clarification on what this memory is \emph{not}: it is not an autonomous learning system. The agent does not write to memory on its own initiative; the author writes every entry, the agent obeys. The persistence mechanism is what compounds the corrections, not any agentic learning capability.

\section{Agent-Driven Multi-API Evaluation}
\label{sec:agent-eval}
% ============================================================================

The evaluation in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} was not conducted manually. All four arms---controlled defect-injection, large-scale APIs.guru, operational deployment, and the agent-feedback experiment---were designed by the author. The APIs.guru arm, the focus of this section, was tooled and executed through an agentic workflow during Phase~B and analysed jointly.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Tooling Development}
\label{subsec:tooling-dev}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The three-step batch testing pipeline (\path{tools/harvest-openapi-apisguru.py}, \path{tools/probe-openapi-endpoints.py}, \path{tools/run-openapi-batch.sh}) was developed collaboratively: the author specified the experimental protocol (harvest from APIs.guru, filter for reachable endpoints, validate in both minimal and strict modes) and reviewed every script before commit; the agent implemented the tooling under that specification. Reachability filtering is necessary even though the static specification is the object of validation: it excludes abandoned registry entries so the reported pass rates reflect currently operated services, and it is a precondition for P006--P007, which require a responding host.

This tooling enabled evaluation at a scale impractical manually, producing the per-principle pass rates reported in Section~\ref{sec:wild-eval} of Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Agent as Experimenter}
\label{subsec:agent-experimenter}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The agentic workflow extended beyond tooling development. The agent executed the evaluation runs, drafted summary tables of the results, and identified candidate patterns (e.g.\ the universal gap between P001 pass rates and P002--P004 pass rates). The author reviewed every analysis, verified the numbers against raw output, refined the interpretation, and is responsible for every numerical claim in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}. The agent did not interpret the results in the thesis; the author did.

This workflow illustrates a key property of SDT's structured output: the same structured, machine-readable, deterministic reports that suit CI/CD quality gates also suit agent-driven evaluation under human supervision. The Axiom of Observability (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}) is what enables this workflow.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Limit Acknowledged: Coverage of Per-Check Cases}
\label{subsec:eval-coverage-limit}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Exercising only one or two cases per principle would leave most of the check matrix untested. The controlled evaluation in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} therefore extends beyond whole-principle defects to per-check injection---two to three targeted mutations per implemented principle, fifteen in total, plus selected combinations of two and three simultaneous defects. This samples the check space rather than exhausting it (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} records complete per-check coverage as future evaluation work), but it demonstrates that detection accuracy holds across every principle at the granularity of individual checks. The injection scheme---which defect at which check, with what expected verdict---was specified by the author; the agent implemented the injection harness under that specification, and the author reviewed every injected defect and verified every verdict before accepting the runs.

\section{Agent-Driven Thesis Writing}
\label{sec:agent-thesis}
% ============================================================================

The crucial fact first: the agent's contribution to the thesis was confined to initial, code-grounded drafts; after each draft the author took over completely, fully rewriting substantive sections, so the final content of every chapter is the author's. Within that model, the Phase~B agentic workflow divided labour as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Author}: chapter structure (the eleven-chapter outline of \texttt{thesis/main.tex}), every research question, every contribution, every figure design, every claim that maps to a specific code element, every numerical result, and the final edited form of every chapter.
    \item \textbf{Agent}: first drafts of prose paragraphs against author-supplied chapter specifications and the feed map; LaTeX formatting; cross-reference plumbing; mechanical edits (e.g.\ the SDT renaming pass).
    \item \textbf{Both}: identification of inconsistencies between thesis claims and code reality. The agent could query the codebase faster, but the author judged whether each flagged inconsistency was a thesis error, a code error, or an acceptable simplification.
\end{itemize}

The thesis CLAUDE.md file (202 lines) provides chapter-by-chapter specifications that function as agent task descriptions: expected sections, source files to read, and key content requirements, with the Complete Feed Map mapping every thesis section to its code and documentation sources. When drafting a section the agent follows a structured protocol:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Read the specification}: the chapter entry in the thesis CLAUDE.md.
    \item \textbf{Read the source}: the code and documentation files named in the feed map.
    \item \textbf{Draft the content}: LaTeX grounded in the actual implementation as the authoritative source.
    \item \textbf{Author takeover}: the author rewrites substantive sections, edits for accuracy and tone, and accepts or rejects.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Thesis as specification: a worked example.} The thesis statement that ``DriveBy implements seven of nine principles as \texttt{PrincipleChecker} instances, with P006 and P007 as planned wrappers'' is treated as authoritative: the CI documentation check cross-references it against the actual files in \texttt{driveby-cli/\allowbreak internal/principles/}, and if a P006 file appeared in source without a corresponding thesis-status update, the build would fail. The thesis is thus the source of truth for the project's claimed scope, and the code is checked against it---more limited than treating the entire thesis as formal specification, but formalising one claim per chapter as a CI-enforced invariant.

\section{SDT as Agent Feedback Infrastructure}
\label{sec:ddt-agent-feedback}
% ============================================================================

The experience of building DriveBy with an AI agent supports a claim made throughout this thesis: that SDT's structured output is naturally suited for consumption by autonomous agents. This section formalises the connection and notes its empirical status.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Agent Validation Loop}
\label{subsec:agent-loop}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Consider an autonomous agent tasked with improving an API specification's SDT compliance. The agent executes the following loop:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Validate}: run \texttt{driveby validate-only --validation-mode strict}.
    \item \textbf{Parse}: read the JSON report; identify failing principles and check failures.
    \item \textbf{Diagnose}: read each failure's \texttt{SuggestedFix} field and \texttt{Details} map.
    \item \textbf{Remediate}: generate specification changes addressing the diagnosed issues---missing descriptions (P002), error responses (P003), schema constraints (P004), security schemes (P005).
    \item \textbf{Re-validate}: run DriveBy again to confirm the fix without new failures.
\end{enumerate}

This loop is feasible because of three properties guaranteed by SDT's axioms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Completeness} ensures the report identifies \textit{what} is missing: a P002 failure listing ``44 request/response bodies missing examples'' gives the agent a precise remediation target.
    \item \textbf{Determinism} lets the agent distinguish genuine fixes from noise: on re-run, a P002 pass confirms the fix; a continued failure indicates it was incomplete.
    \item \textbf{Observability} ensures the report is structured and machine-readable---the agent reads fields, not prose.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Empirical Evidence and Open Validation}
\label{sec:agent-feedback-future}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The theoretical connection above between SDT feedback and agent corrections requires empirical demonstration; the development experience alone is indirect evidence---it shows SDT's output is consumable by an agent under human supervision, but not that an agent can autonomously improve a specification's SDT score using only the validation report as input.

Such an experiment is reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} (Section~\ref{sec:sdt-feedback-experiment}): the Swagger Petstore specification---a real-world public API scoring 1/6 in strict mode---is taken as the starting point, the DriveBy validation report is supplied as the sole input to an AI agent, and the agent's revised specification is re-validated. A single agent-revision pass raised the score from 1/6 to 4/6, converting the theoretical connection of this section into an empirical one. The author considers a more comprehensive version of this experiment (multiple agents, multiple specification revisions per API, controlled comparison against human-revised specifications) to be an important direction for follow-up doctoral work (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\subsection{From Validation to Remediation}
\label{subsec:validation-remediation}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The \texttt{SuggestedFix} field in each \texttt{PrincipleResult} is the interface between validation and remediation: it names the specific operations, schemas, fields, or security definitions at fault. These fixes are not aspirational---in the agent-feedback experiment (Section~\ref{sec:sdt-feedback-experiment}) the agent translated the \texttt{SuggestedFix} entries of the baseline Petstore report into concrete edits: descriptions and examples (P002), 4xx/5xx error responses wired to all 19 operations (P003), schema constraints (P004), security schemes (P005), and versioning metadata (P008). When DriveBy reports ``string field \texttt{email} lacks length constraints,'' the remediation is unambiguous: add \texttt{maxLength} and \texttt{pattern} to the field's schema, derivable from the report without external documentation, a human, or inferred intent.

That the validation report contains sufficient information for autonomous remediation is the Axiom of Completeness applied to the validation output rather than the specification input; the \texttt{SuggestedFix} field ensures it.

\textbf{A limitation: invented constraint values.} One caveat tempers the remediation claim. An agent that supplies concrete constraint values---a \texttt{maxLength} bound, a \texttt{pattern} regular expression---without grounding them in the actual implementation can produce a specification that \emph{passes} the principles while \emph{misrepresenting} the API's real behaviour. The divergence is not necessarily detectable even by functional testing (the implementation may accept inputs the invented constraint forbids), and such a specification would mislead API clients. Agent-remediated specifications must therefore be verified against the implementation by its owners before promotion to production; automating this verification (e.g.\ cross-checking proposed constraints against observed traffic or implementation code) is left as future work.

\section{Personal Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:personal-lessons}
% ============================================================================

Whereas lessons about the agentic-development \emph{framework} generalise (Sections~\ref{sec:claudemd-protocol} and~\ref{sec:agent-memory}; Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}), this section records what \emph{the author} learned from this specific experience.

\textbf{The 350-day solo investment was decisive, not the 12-day partnership.} The AI partnership only became productive because of the year of solo work that preceded it: without the SDT axioms, the principle catalogue, the \texttt{APISpec} abstraction, the code-quality rules, and the monorepo layout already in place, Phase~B would have produced architecturally inconsistent code. In a follow-up project the author would again resist engaging AI assistance early. Could the agent also have supplied design ideas worth adopting? In this project it did not: the division of labour was deliberately strict---architect and executor---all architectural and design decisions originated with the author, and the agent's suggestions stayed at implementation-level tactics, none adopted as a design-level idea (Section~\ref{subsec:authorship-claim}).

\textbf{Persisting corrections is the highest-leverage skill.} The behaviour that made the largest difference was treating every recurring agent mistake as a CLAUDE.md or memory entry rather than a one-shot correction: a correction that lives in a chat session dies with the session; one that lives in a file compounds.

\textbf{Code review remained the bottleneck.} The agent could produce more output than the author could verify; the verification rate was the rate-limit. In a follow-up project the author would budget explicit verification time---reviewing the previous session's output to completion before starting the next.

\textbf{The thesis benefited from being treated as one of the artefacts.} Drafting the thesis with the same protocol used for code (chapter specifications, feed map, CI documentation check) produced tighter alignment between document and implementation than a separate writing process would have; the author would adopt this pattern by default in follow-up projects.

\textbf{What the author would do differently.} Three specific changes for a follow-up project are planned: (i)~record exact wall-clock time per activity from day one (so that questions like ``how much time did you spend reviewing AI output?'' have measured rather than estimated answers); (ii)~set up the controlled-experiment harness for the SDT-feedback evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:agent-feedback-future}) before starting Phase~B, so that the empirical proof of the framework's most consequential claim is part of the deliverable from the start; (iii)~establish the precision/accuracy axiom debate (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}) earlier, so that the framework is presented as four-axioms-with-precision rather than three-axioms-with-precision-as-future-work.

\textbf{Not every lesson is AI-specific.} Three transferable practices carry forward irrespective of agentic tooling: fixing architectural invariants up front as non-negotiable constraints; treating documentation as a CI-gated, testable artefact (the practice SDT itself generalises); and maintaining authorship traceability through disciplined commit metadata, which made the quantitative analysis of Section~\ref{subsec:quant-authorship} possible at all.
