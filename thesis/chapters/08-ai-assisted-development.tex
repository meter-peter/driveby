\chapter{AI-Assisted Development: Authorship, Process, and Reflection}
\label{ch:ai-development}

This thesis was conceived, designed, evaluated, and written by the author. It was \emph{built} with substantial help from an AI agent during a focused twelve-day productisation sprint near the end of the project. This chapter draws that distinction precisely. It opens with a self-assessment of authorship and the author's role (Section~\ref{sec:authorship}), then describes the knowledge protocol and persistent memory that made the human--agent collaboration tractable (Sections~\ref{sec:claudemd-protocol} and~\ref{sec:agent-memory}), and closes with a reflection on what worked, what would change in a follow-on project, and how the framework defined in earlier chapters relates to autonomous-agent feedback loops (Sections~\ref{sec:agent-eval}--\ref{sec:personal-lessons}).

The chapter makes three claims. First, that the author's intellectual ownership---over the SDT framework, the XSDLC architecture, the evaluation design, and the thesis narrative---is unambiguous and traceable through the version-control record. Second, that the CLAUDE.md knowledge protocol the author designed is the enabling infrastructure that allowed an AI agent to accelerate implementation without dictating direction. Third, that SDT's structured, machine-readable output positions it as a natural feedback source for future autonomous-agent loops---an observation grounded in the experience of this project and to be tested empirically as future work (Section~\ref{sec:agent-feedback-future}).

\subsection{The Two Phases of Development}
\label{subsec:two-phases}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The repository's commit history makes a clean distinction between two phases.

\textbf{Phase~A --- Solo development (3 April 2025 -- 18 March 2026, 350 days).} During this phase the author worked alone. The DDT/SDT axioms were drafted, the nine principles were enumerated, the \texttt{APISpec} abstraction was designed, the first principle checkers were written, the OpenAPI~3.x and Swagger~2.0 adapters were built, the validation modes were specified, the monorepo was restructured, the five evaluation APIs were designed and implemented, and the Crossplane composition was prototyped. All twenty-six commits in this phase carry no \texttt{Co-Authored-By} trailer; they are entirely the author's work.

\textbf{Phase~B --- Author-directed AI partnership (19--30 March 2026, 12 days).} During this phase the author engaged Claude Code intensively for productisation: the GitOps pipeline was wired together, OSS-polish cleanup was performed, the multi-API evaluation tooling was developed, the XSDLC composition was hardened, and the thesis manuscript was drafted under the author's chapter specifications and feed map (Section~\ref{sec:agent-thesis}). Twenty-six of the fifty-two commits in the project carry the \texttt{Co-Authored-By: Claude} trailer; all of them fall in this 12-day window. Crucially, human-only commits continued throughout this phase too---the last human-only commit (2026-03-29) was made one day before the final commit---answering directly the supervisor's question about whether the author kept making manual code changes in later commits.

This timeline matters. The conceptual content of the thesis---the axioms, the principles, the architecture, the evaluation design---predates the AI partnership by nearly a year. The AI's role was not to invent the framework; it was to accelerate the implementation and writing of a framework that already existed in design.

\section{The CLAUDE.md Knowledge Protocol}
\label{sec:claudemd-protocol}
% ============================================================================

The central enabling infrastructure for the Phase~B partnership---and the author's most reusable methodological contribution from the agentic-development framework (Contribution~6 in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction})---is a hierarchical system of \texttt{CLAUDE.md} files distributed across the monorepo. These files are not READMEs and not configuration. They are agent context files: structured knowledge artifacts designed to be consumed by AI agents at the start of every working session, providing the persistent context that enables cross-session continuity.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

When an agent begins working in a directory, the CLAUDE.md files in the path from root to that directory are loaded into the agent's context. An agent modifying \texttt{driveby-cli/internal/principles/p004\_schema.go} receives context from three CLAUDE.md files: the root (project-wide conventions), the CLI (package structure), and the principles directory (interface contract, axiom assignments). This layered loading ensures that the agent always has both the global invariants and the local domain knowledge needed for the task at hand.

\textbf{Hierarchy depth and rationale.} The hierarchy is three levels deep at most (root $\to$ package $\to$ component) because deeper nesting exceeded the practical context budget per session and produced redundant content. The depth was chosen empirically by the author during Phase~A: an initial flat structure produced agent sessions that lacked package-level context, and an initial five-level structure produced redundant content that the agent's context window could not absorb without truncation. The three-level depth that the project settled on was a deliberate engineering trade-off, not an arbitrary one.

\textbf{Containment vs.\ repetition.} Some sub-directory CLAUDE.md files repeat invariants from the root file. The supervisor noted~([534]) that repetition is in tension with the layered-loading model. The author's choice was to repeat \emph{critical} invariants (the five code-quality rules) in the leaf files where they are most relevant, on the principle that an agent operating under context-window pressure may truncate ancestor content but is unlikely to truncate the file it is actively editing. The cost is duplication; the benefit is robustness against context-window truncation. This is a defensible engineering choice rather than a methodological mistake.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Content Categories}
\label{subsec:content-categories}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Each CLAUDE.md file contains four categories of knowledge:

\textbf{Structural knowledge}: what exists, where it lives, and how it connects. The root CLAUDE.md contains the monorepo directory table with status indicators. The CLI CLAUDE.md contains the package dependency graph. The principles CLAUDE.md contains a per-file table with line counts, axiom assignments, and implementation status---one row per principle file.

\textbf{Invariants}: rules that must never be violated. The root file specifies the five code quality rules listed in Section~\ref{subsec:quant-authorship}: (1)~the mandatory \texttt{spec.APISpec} abstraction; (2)~the \texttt{PrincipleChecker} interface for every checker; (3)~no \texttt{os.Exit()} in command handlers; (4)~types confined to \texttt{internal/types/} with no circular dependencies; (5)~CLI configuration shared via \texttt{config.go} with no duplication. These invariants are repeated in the sub-directory CLAUDE.md files where they are most relevant, ensuring that an agent working in a leaf directory still encounters the project-wide rules.

\textbf{Procedures}: step-by-step instructions for common tasks. The principles CLAUDE.md includes the procedure for adding a new principle (create source file, test file, docs entry, update CLAUDE.md). The root file includes procedures for building, testing, and releasing. These procedures transform tribal knowledge (``ask Peter how to add a new principle'') into structured, machine-executable instructions.

\textbf{Thesis mapping}: bidirectional traceability between code and academic writing. Every sub-directory CLAUDE.md includes a Thesis Mapping section identifying which thesis chapter describes that code. The thesis CLAUDE.md includes a Complete Feed Map table mapping every thesis section to the code files and documentation files the agent should read before writing. This bidirectional mapping enables two critical workflows: updating thesis content when code changes, and updating code when thesis analysis reveals inconsistencies.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Maintenance Rules and Self-Enforcement}
\label{subsec:maintenance-rules}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The CLAUDE.md protocol includes four maintenance rules encoded in the root file:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item When modifying source files, update the corresponding CLAUDE.md in that directory.
    \item When modifying source files, update the corresponding \texttt{docs/} file if documented behavior changes.
    \item When adding a new principle, create: source file, test file, \texttt{docs/principles/PXXX.md}, and update \texttt{principles/CLAUDE.md}.
    \item When adding a new directory, create a CLAUDE.md in it and add it to the root monorepo table.
\end{enumerate}

The fourth rule deserves a clarification. Directories in this project were planned by the author up-front during the monorepo restructuring at the start of Phase~B; very few directories were created reactively by the agent during the partnership. The rule was therefore exercised more often as a verification step (``did the agent forget to add a CLAUDE.md to a new directory it created?'') than as a frequent operational requirement. It would matter more in projects where AI agents drive the directory layout, which was not the case here.

These rules are not merely advisory. The project's CI pipeline includes a documentation completeness check (\texttt{tools/check-docs.sh}) that mechanically verifies CLAUDE.md coverage. The script performs nine categories of checks: CLAUDE.md existence in required directories, principle documentation completeness, documentation-versus-reality consistency (detecting stale claims like ``Not yet implemented'' when the source file exists), thesis chapter status (flagging stubs under 20 lines), and test coverage gaps. When any check fails, the CI build fails.

This CI enforcement closes the loop. The CLAUDE.md protocol tells agents what to update; the CI check verifies that updates were made. The combination prevents knowledge drift---the same problem that SDT prevents for API specifications. In both cases, the solution is the same: treat documentation as a testable artifact and gate on its completeness.

\section{Persistent Agent Memory}
\label{sec:agent-memory}
% ============================================================================

Beyond the per-directory CLAUDE.md files, the project maintains a persistent memory system that survives across agent sessions. This memory captures information that cannot be derived from the codebase: user preferences, learned corrections, project decisions, and external references.

\textbf{Category of memory system.} In the taxonomy of memory architectures for AI systems, this falls under \emph{external, file-backed, semantic} memory: external (state lives outside the agent process, in files on disk), file-backed (each entry is a Markdown file with frontmatter, version-controllable independently), and semantic (entries store generalised knowledge---rules, preferences, project facts---rather than verbatim conversation transcripts). It is not episodic memory (no per-session conversation logs are persisted in this directory) and not procedural memory (procedures live in the CLAUDE.md files, not here). This addresses the supervisor's request~([544]) to name the category explicitly.

The memory directory contains twelve files: an index file (\texttt{MEMORY.md}) and eleven content entries.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{User profile} (1 file): the developer's role (diploma thesis student), expertise (deep Go and Kubernetes, new to LaTeX), and communication preferences.
    \item \textbf{Feedback corrections} (8 files): learned behaviours from iterative interaction. Examples include \texttt{feedback\_docs\_updates} (``always update CLAUDE.md, docs, and thesis drafts after each implementation session''), \texttt{feedback\_ask\_questions} (``don't assume; ask meaningful questions when decisions are ambiguous''), \texttt{feedback\_save\_test\_results} (``every DriveBy test result must be saved as pretty-printed JSON in \texttt{results/}''), and \texttt{feedback\_runtime\_spec} (``sample APIs generate specs at runtime, not from static \texttt{openapi.json}''---a correction made after the agent assumed file-backed specs).
    \item \textbf{Project decisions} (1 file): the monorepo restructuring rationale.
    \item \textbf{External references} (1 file): the target cluster URL and the instruction to never reinstall ArgoCD.
\end{itemize}

The feedback corrections are the most active part of the system. Each correction encodes a lesson learned from a failed interaction: the agent did something wrong, the user corrected it, and the correction was persisted so that the same mistake would not recur in future sessions. Over the 12-day Phase~B partnership these corrections accumulated into a behavioural profile that shaped the agent's approach to the project. This is analogous to organisational learning in human teams~\cite{nonaka-knowledge}: tacit knowledge (``Peter wants docs updated after every session'') is externalised into explicit, persistent artifacts that new sessions inherit.

A clarification on what this memory is \emph{not}: it is not an autonomous learning system. The agent does not write to memory on its own initiative; the author authors every entry, the agent obeys. The persistence mechanism is what compounds the corrections, not any agentic learning capability.

\section{Agent-Driven Multi-API Evaluation}
\label{sec:agent-eval}
% ============================================================================

The evaluation described in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} was not conducted manually. The large-scale evaluation against APIs.guru was designed by the author, tooled and executed through an agentic workflow during Phase~B, and analysed jointly.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Tooling Development}
\label{subsec:tooling-dev}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The three-step batch testing pipeline (\texttt{tools/harvest-openapi-apisguru.py}, \texttt{tools/probe-openapi-endpoints.py}, \texttt{tools/run-openapi-batch.sh}) was developed collaboratively. The author specified the experimental protocol (harvest from APIs.guru, filter for reachable endpoints, validate in both minimal and strict modes) and reviewed every script before it was committed; the agent implemented the tooling under that specification. The harvester script queries the APIs.guru registry, filters for OpenAPI 3.x specifications, and produces a CSV of API names, specification URLs, and base hosts. The prober validates reachability by testing simple GET endpoints. The batch runner executes DriveBy against each entry and collects results.

This tooling enabled evaluation at a scale that would have been impractical manually. The batch runner processed APIs from major providers (Stripe, Twilio, GitHub, AWS) alongside smaller independent APIs, producing per-principle pass rates that revealed the systematic documentation quality gaps reported in Section~\ref{sec:wild-eval} of Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Agent as Experimenter}
\label{subsec:agent-experimenter}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The agentic workflow extended beyond tooling development. The agent executed the evaluation runs, drafted summary tables of the results, and identified candidate patterns (e.g., the universal gap between P001 pass rates and P002--P004 pass rates). The author reviewed every analysis, verified the numbers against raw output, refined the interpretation, and is responsible for every numerical claim in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}. The agent did not interpret the results in the thesis; the author did.

This workflow illustrates a key property of SDT's structured output. The validation reports produced by DriveBy are JSON documents with per-principle pass/fail status, specific check results, and suggested remediations. An AI agent can parse these reports, identify patterns across multiple APIs, and generate comparative analysis under human supervision. The same property that makes SDT output suitable for CI/CD quality gates---structured, machine-readable, deterministic---makes it suitable for agent-driven evaluation. The Axiom of Observability, which requires that validation produces measurable, actionable results, is what enables this workflow.

\section{Agent-Driven Thesis Writing}
\label{sec:agent-thesis}
% ============================================================================

This thesis document was produced through an agentic workflow during Phase~B, but with a clear division of labour between the author and the agent. The supervisor's expectation~([549]) that the thesis was written by the author---with the agent serving as a checker against the codebase rather than as the primary writer---is partially correct but not the full picture. The actual division was:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Author}: chapter structure (the ten-chapter outline of \texttt{thesis/main.tex}), every research question, every contribution, every figure design, every claim that maps to a specific code element, every numerical result, and the final edited form of every chapter.
    \item \textbf{Agent}: first drafts of prose paragraphs against author-supplied chapter specifications and the feed map; LaTeX formatting; cross-reference plumbing; mechanical edits (e.g.\ the SDT renaming pass after supervisor review).
    \item \textbf{Both}: identification of inconsistencies between thesis claims and code reality. The agent could query the codebase faster, but the author judged whether each flagged inconsistency was a thesis error, a code error, or an acceptable simplification.
\end{itemize}

The thesis CLAUDE.md file (171 lines) provides chapter-by-chapter specifications that function as agent task descriptions: for each chapter, it lists the expected sections, the source code files to read, the documentation files to consult, and the key content requirements. The Complete Feed Map table maps every thesis section to both its code and documentation sources. When the agent drafts a thesis section, it follows a structured protocol:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Read the specification}: the thesis CLAUDE.md's chapter entry identifies what the chapter should contain.
    \item \textbf{Read the source}: the feed map identifies which code files and documentation files to read for factual accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Draft the content}: the agent produces LaTeX content grounded in the actual implementation, using the code as the authoritative source.
    \item \textbf{Author review}: the author reads the draft, edits for accuracy and tone, and accepts or rejects. Substantive sections were fully rewritten by the author after the draft.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Thesis as specification: a worked example.} The supervisor asked~([542]) for a concrete example justifying the claim that the thesis can be treated as a kind of specification against which the code is checked. One representative case: the thesis statement that ``DriveBy implements seven of nine principles as \texttt{PrincipleChecker} instances, with P006 and P007 as planned wrappers'' is treated as authoritative. The CI documentation check (\texttt{tools/check-docs.sh}) cross-references this against the actual files in \texttt{driveby-cli/\allowbreak internal/principles/}: if a P006 file appeared in source without a corresponding update to the thesis status, the build would fail. In this sense the thesis is treated as a high-level specification that gates code state---an asymmetric relationship where the thesis is the source of truth for the project's claimed scope, and the code is checked against it. This is more limited than treating the entire thesis as formal specification, but it does formalise one specific claim per chapter as a CI-enforced invariant.

\section{SDT as Agent Feedback Infrastructure}
\label{sec:ddt-agent-feedback}
% ============================================================================

The experience of building DriveBy with an AI agent over Phase~B supports a claim made throughout this thesis: that SDT's structured output is naturally suited for consumption by autonomous agents. This section formalises the connection and notes its current empirical status.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Agent Validation Loop}
\label{subsec:agent-loop}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Consider an autonomous agent tasked with improving an API specification's SDT compliance. The agent executes the following loop:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Validate}: Run \texttt{driveby validate-only --validation-mode strict} against the specification.
    \item \textbf{Parse}: Read the JSON report. Identify failing principles and specific check failures.
    \item \textbf{Diagnose}: For each failure, read the \texttt{SuggestedFix} field and the \texttt{Details} map to determine what is missing or incorrect.
    \item \textbf{Remediate}: Generate specification changes that address the diagnosed issues---add missing descriptions (P002), document error responses (P003), add schema constraints (P004), define security schemes (P005).
    \item \textbf{Re-validate}: Run DriveBy again to confirm that the remediation resolved the issue without introducing new failures.
\end{enumerate}

This loop is feasible because of three properties guaranteed by SDT's axioms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Completeness} ensures that the validation report identifies \textit{what} is missing. A P002 failure with a details map listing ``44 request/response bodies missing examples'' gives the agent a precise remediation target.
    \item \textbf{Determinism} ensures that the agent can distinguish genuine fixes from noise. If the agent adds examples and re-runs validation, a P002 pass confirms the fix; a continued failure indicates the fix was incomplete. Non-deterministic validation would make this feedback loop unreliable.
    \item \textbf{Observability} ensures that the report is structured and machine-readable. The JSON format with per-principle results, named checks, and suggested fixes is parseable without natural language understanding---the agent reads fields, not prose.
\end{itemize}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Empirical Evidence and Open Validation}
\label{sec:agent-feedback-future}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The supervisor noted~([550]) that the theoretical connection above between SDT feedback and agent corrections is well-argued but lacks an empirical demonstration in the current evaluation. The author accepts this critique. The evidence currently in this thesis is indirect: the development of DriveBy itself shows that SDT's output is consumable by an agent under human supervision, but the thesis does not yet contain a controlled experiment in which an agent autonomously improves an API specification's SDT score using only the validation report as input.

Such an experiment is added as a new sub-section of Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} (Section~\ref{sec:sdt-feedback-experiment}): one or two real-world public APIs with low P002--P004 scores are taken as starting points, the DriveBy validation report is supplied as the sole input to an AI agent, and the agent's revised specification is re-validated to measure the score delta. The result of this experiment converts the theoretical connection of this section into an empirical one. The author considers a more comprehensive version of this experiment (multiple agents, multiple specification revisions per API, controlled comparison against human-revised specifications) to be an important direction for follow-on doctoral work (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\subsection{From Validation to Remediation}
\label{subsec:validation-remediation}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The \texttt{SuggestedFix} field in each \texttt{PrincipleResult} is the interface between validation and remediation. For P002 failures, the suggested fix identifies specific operations lacking descriptions and specific schemas lacking examples. For P004 failures, it identifies specific fields lacking type constraints. For P005 failures, it identifies missing security scheme definitions.

These suggested fixes are not aspirational---they are actionable specifications that an agent can translate into concrete specification edits. When DriveBy reports ``string field \texttt{email} lacks length constraints,'' the remediation is unambiguous: add \texttt{maxLength} and \texttt{pattern} properties to the field's schema. The specification change is derivable from the failure report without consulting external documentation, querying a human, or inferring intent from context.

This property---that the validation report contains sufficient information for autonomous remediation---is a direct consequence of the Axiom of Completeness applied to the validation output rather than the specification input. Just as SDT requires the specification to be complete enough for validation, SDT's own output must be complete enough for remediation. The \texttt{SuggestedFix} field ensures this.

\section{Personal Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:personal-lessons}
% ============================================================================

The two preceding generations of ``lessons learned'' material in earlier drafts of this chapter conflated two different things: lessons about the agentic-development \emph{framework} (which generalise) and lessons \emph{the author} learned (which are specific to this experience). The supervisor asked~([528], part~c) for the latter, distinct from the former. This section provides them.

\textbf{The 350-day solo investment was decisive, not the 12-day partnership.} The most useful single piece of self-feedback is that the AI partnership only became productive because of the year of solo work that preceded it. Without the SDT axioms, the principle catalogue, the \texttt{APISpec} abstraction, the five code-quality rules, and the monorepo layout already in place, Phase~B would have produced architecturally inconsistent code. In a follow-on project, the author would resist the temptation to engage AI assistance early; the up-front design work is the thing that makes later acceleration possible.

\textbf{Persisting corrections is the highest-leverage skill.} The single behaviour that made the largest difference during Phase~B was treating every recurring agent mistake as a CLAUDE.md or memory entry rather than as a one-shot correction. A correction that lives in a chat session dies with the session; a correction that lives in a file compounds. In a follow-on project, the author would invest more effort earlier in writing this kind of structured feedback rather than re-explaining the same constraint three times.

\textbf{Code review remained the bottleneck.} During Phase~B, the agent could produce more output than the author could verify. The output rate was a temptation; the verification rate was the rate-limit. The author would in a follow-on project budget time explicitly for verification before each session---e.g.\ committing to review the previous session's output to completion before starting the next session, even if that means a quieter sprint.

\textbf{The thesis benefited from being treated as one of the artifacts.} Drafting the thesis with the same protocol used for code (chapter specifications, feed map, CI documentation check) produced a tighter alignment between the document and the implementation than a separate writing process would have. In follow-on projects the author would adopt this pattern by default---treat the thesis or final report as a first-class artifact in the development pipeline from the start, rather than as a downstream deliverable to be written at the end.

\textbf{What the author would do differently.} Three specific changes for a follow-on project: (i)~record exact wall-clock time per activity from day one (so that questions like ``how much time did you spend reviewing AI output?'' have measured rather than estimated answers); (ii)~set up the controlled-experiment harness for the SDT-feedback evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:agent-feedback-future}) before starting Phase~B, so that the empirical proof of the framework's most consequential claim is part of the deliverable from the start; (iii)~establish the precision/accuracy axiom debate (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}) earlier, so that the framework is presented as four-axioms-with-precision rather than three-axioms-with-precision-as-future-work.

These are the author's lessons, not the agent's. They are what the experience of running this project taught the author about how to run the next one.
